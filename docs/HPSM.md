## PREAMBLE

HPSM is the human stability architecture of AVCS. It implements the structural integrity principles defined in the **AVCS Charter v1.1** and **CORE v2.1**. It operates under the ethical framework of the **Code of Ethics v1.1** and the technical standard of the **Code of Practice v1.1**.

Where HPSM and the Charter appear to conflict, the **Charter prevails**.

HPSM does not replace human judgment. It protects the conditions under which human judgment remains possible.

## 1. WHY HPSM EXISTS

Operations do not collapse because people are weak.

They collapse because environments systematically erode human cognitive stability through:

- information overload,
- responsibility pressure,
- ambiguity,
- fatigue,
- time-critical decision pressure,
- fragmented support systems.

Technology today mostly makes this worse.

HPSM exists to do the opposite:

**To stabilize the human system so humans remain strong, calm, and capable.**

## 2. CORE PHILOSOPHY

HPSM is built on a simple truth:

**Machinery fails mechanically.**
**Humans fail cognitively.**

So we do not only engineer steel, electronics, and data systems.

**We engineer human performance conditions.**

### 2.1 Evidence Before Status

HPSM does not accept a claimed human condition — "Ready," "Fit for Duty," "Not Impaired" — as evidence of a structural condition.

For every claim of human readiness, HPSM requires evidence that existed **at or before the moment** of the assessed act.

For every critical human state, three things must be independently establishable:

1. The **claimed status** — what the record says.
2. The **required structural condition** — what must actually be true.
3. The **evidence** — what can be independently verified.

If the condition behind the status cannot be shown, the human state is **not structural**.

This principle is encoded in:

- Charter v1.1, Section 4.3
- CORE v2.1, Law 21
- Code of Ethics v1.1, Section 11
- SIM v1.1, Section 2.3
- INS v1.1, Section 4

## 3. THE FOUR PILLARS OF HUMAN PERFORMANCE STABILITY

HPSM evaluates four structural pillars. Each pillar must be testable, measurable, and auditable.

### Pillar 1 — Cognitive Clarity

**Definition:** Humans perform best when signals are clear, priorities are visible, risk is explicit, and noise is filtered.

**Question:** *Is the signal clear and action mandatory?*

**Responsibilities:**

- ensure signals are clear,
- ensure priorities are visible,
- ensure risk is explicit,
- ensure noise is filtered.

**Outcome:** Higher decision speed + higher decision accuracy.

**Failure Pattern:**
Signals lost in noise → decision delay → error under pressure.

**Diagnostic Questions:**

- Is risk information presented without extraneous data?
- How many alarms per hour are actionable?
- Is the signal clear, and is the action mandatory?
- Does the system require interpretation or activation?

**Evidence Required:**

- alarm design documentation,
- signal-to-action pathways,
- decision-support interfaces,
- records of decision delays.

> **Clarity without authority is frustration.**

### Pillar 2 — Load Balance

**Definition:** Performance collapses when mental load exceeds capacity.

**Question:** *Is workload distributed?*

**Responsibilities:**

- stabilize mental workload through structured guidance,
- provide shared reasoning frameworks,
- provide decision scaffolding,
- provide progressive support layers.

**Outcome:** Sustained clarity under pressure.

**Failure Pattern:**
Cognitive overload → decision paralysis → error under pressure.

**Diagnostic Questions:**

- Is workload distributed?
- Do operators report feeling cognitively overloaded?
- Are there structural alternatives when workload exceeds capacity?
- Does the system track patterns of operator unfitness (fatigue, illness, intoxication, mental distress)?

**Evidence Required:**

- workload assessment data,
- staffing records,
- shift rotation documentation,
- operator fitness records.

> **No one should be overloaded under pressure.**

### Pillar 3 — Reliability Conditioning

**Definition:** People do not become reliable because they "try harder."

They become reliable because they:

- operate inside structured logic,
- see consequences clearly,
- gain confidence from stable systems,
- trust their environment.

**Question:** *Do we act by structure or by hope?*

**Responsibilities:**

- design structured logic,
- make consequences visible,
- build stable systems,
- build trustable environments.

**Outcome:** Fewer mistakes, stronger discipline, higher resilience.

**Failure Pattern:**
Workarounds → hidden risk → normalization of deviation.

**Diagnostic Questions:**

- Do operators understand the consequences of their decisions?
- Do operators trust the system, or maintain workarounds?
- Are informal workarounds normalized?
- Is reliability expected to come from individual effort or structural design?

**Evidence Required:**

- workaround documentation,
- trust surveys,
- consequence-awareness records,
- structural reliability metrics.

> **People do not become reliable by trying harder.**

### Pillar 4 — Responsibility Integrity

**Definition:** Authority and accountability must match.

**Question:** *Does authority match responsibility?*

**Responsibilities:**

- ensure human authority matches human accountability,
- ensure technology supports human responsibility instead of replacing it,
- ensure those who see the problem can act.

**Outcome:**

- leaders stay leaders,
- operators stay in control,
- trust remains intact.

**Failure Pattern:**
Authority mismatch → disengagement → hidden risk.

**Diagnostic Questions:**

- Do operators feel they have authority matching their accountability?
- Can technology override human decisions without explanation?
- Can those who see a problem act on it?
- Are responsibility and authority aligned?

**Evidence Required:**

- authority matrices,
- decision-rights documentation,
- override logs,
- intervention records.

> **Those who see the problem must be able to act.**

## 4. OPERATOR FITNESS PROTECTION

HPSM recognizes that human performance depends on conditions that may be structurally compromised:

- fatigue (lack of sleep, long hours),
- illness (fever, dizziness, medication that impairs),
- intoxication (alcohol, drugs, hangover),
- mental distress (acute stress, trauma, emotional state).

HPSM requires that the architecture under assessment includes protected mechanisms for an operator to refuse duty when unfit.

The diagnostic questions are:

1. Does the system provide a formal, protected mechanism for an operator to refuse duty when unfit?
2. What happens to an operator who refuses duty due to being unfit?
3. Are there structural alternatives (standby crew, shift rotation, relief pool) when an operator is unfit?
4. Is refusal recorded as a safety decision, not as absenteeism or a performance issue?

Where such mechanisms are absent, the absence is reported as a **structural gap** — not as an individual performance issue.

This principle is encoded in:

- Code of Ethics v1.1, Section 13
- SIM v1.1, Pillar 3 (Protected Intervention)
